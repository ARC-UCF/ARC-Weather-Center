## Synopsis
Ridging across the west-central United States will lend favor to marginally increased probabilities for severe weather, though this threat will remain marginal. This weak ridging across the western portions of the United States combined with no ridging across the eastern United States will contribute to this somewhat increased probability. Low shear, high CAPE scenarios are most likely with any given severe weather threat, where any amount of wind shear is able to contribute to some organization of severe weather. 

Precipitation across the Florida peninsula is leaning slightly above average for this time of year, but not significantly so. This, combined with no ridging across the eastern United States, may contribute to increased rain probabilities. However, within the 108 to 126 hour range, decreased upper-level moisture may prevent rainfall for some periods. Even so, conditions will continue to remain generally hostile for outdoor activity during the mid-afternoon to late evening and to the night hours. 

The El Nino will continue to strengthen. El Nino probabilities per the latest SPC ENSO diagnostics indicate an 80%+ probability of a very strong El Nino by the November/December period. A strong to very strong El Nino may remain present across the western Pacific, and may contribute to increased rainfall probabilities as well as generally cooler than normal weather. The subtropical jet is expected to develop.

## Discussion
A ridging pattern across the western and west-central United States with limited ridging to the east will favor marginally higher probabilities for severe weather during some parts of July. Still increased moisture levels across the peninsula will mean that any wind shear present will result in a low shear, high CAPE environment given that CAPE values will likely remain in the 3000 to 4000 J/Kg range, and DCAPE values will likely remain above 500+ J/Kg, and combined with likely higher PWAT values close to 2" will further contribute to any wind shear being capable of producing strong to severe storms with any particularly dense portions of moisture. This trend could continue to be severe based on observing the recent severe weather threat, where 3 considerable severe thunderstorm warnings were issued in the state of Florida as it related to the four Marginal risk days issued from the 11th of July to today, the 14th of July. 

Actual temperatures will be somewhat lower, but the heat index will generally continue to remain in the 100s, at or around that temperature, for the forseeable future and likely into next month. The dewpoint and relative humidity will continue to remain oppressive, and wind will is not likely to be present during this time to aide in heat relief. This is inferred also from the PWAT, which is expected to remain near 2". It is unlikely significant relief from these temperatures will be felt until at the earliest September. By August, September, and October, we will begin to approach the peak of hurricane season. It is too early to tell what hurricane impacts could occur, if any. 

Based upon these trends, it is likely most days, if not all, will pose a Moderate operational hazard risk, and it is nearly guaranteed most days will warrant a Low operational hazard risk. Scattered to potentially widespread thunderstorm activity will remain prominent threats, with a light precipitation hazard existing after the passage of any thunderstorms. Heat hazards will also likely be present.

El Nino may become a prominent hazard to operations by November and December. The earliest development of a very strong and significant El Nino is event is expected by September, where formation chances are at 48%. By November and December, this El Nino will likely have developed into a very strong and potentially **record-breaking** El Nino, with temperature anomalies potentially reaching into 3 degrees celsius above normal, though it is entirely possible a reach toward 4 degrees could occur. This could cause significant implications for any winter activity, especially with any mid-latitude cyclones which are able to develop in the Gulf and traverse over to the Florida peninsula. 

Although already commonly assosciated with the winter, be aware that this development *could*, though it is not guaranteed, result in an increased wind threat. These surface lows are commonly capable of producing high winds, including winds of 60 mph or greater. Further, emphasis on the cold threat is necessary, as stronger El Ninos are commonly assosciated with cooler air across the southeast. Though it is unlikely we will experience anything to the extent we did in the early January of 2026, it may be possible. The biggest emphasis is necessary for flooding and severe weather. None of this individual outcomes is guaranteed, especially at this time, but they may be generally more likely as a result of the development of the El Nino, especially by the winter months, assuming the current ridging pattern across the western and west-central United States does not persist.
